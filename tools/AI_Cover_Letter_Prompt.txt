PART 1 — INTRODUCTION
The first sentence is a hook: a specific, real statistic tied to the industry, sector, or problem this role sits in. Pick a number that makes the reader stop, and it must be true. Two sources are allowed:
- Something you genuinely read, with the source named plainly: "A [state or national] audit I read found that [stat]."
- Something you learned first-hand on a real project: "While building [specific project], I found that [stat]."
Lead with the stat. It IS the opening. Do not write a warm-up sentence before it.

Then, in the next one or two sentences, turn that stat straight into you: where your relevant expertise comes from, why that makes you the right fit for THIS role, and the scale of impact you can make. A number is best. The job title must appear naturally here for ATS.

The test: the first sentence should read like a fact worth knowing, not a candidate clearing their throat. If the opening could be dropped into a different person's application unchanged, it is wrong.

Register examples — these are the register to aim for. Study the rhythm and plainness, not just the content:
- "A [state or national] audit found most council waste contracts overrun budget inside two years. Closing exactly that kind of gap is what I have spent [X years] doing, which is why the [Job Title] at [Org] fits: [your strongest quantified outcome from the content bank]."

What these examples have in common: the stat lands first, it is specific and checkable, and the second sentence converts it into fit and impact. There is no warm-up sentence. Every word is load-bearing.

STEP 1 — SCAN AND REMOVE ALL AI PATTERNS
Significance inflation — delete every instance:
Words to eliminate: stands as, serves as a testament, pivotal moment, underscores, highlights its importance, marks a shift, setting the stage for, indelible mark, deeply rooted, evolving landscape, focal point, enduring legacy, reflects broader trends, contributing to the

Promotional / advertisement language — delete every instance:
Words to eliminate: boasts, vibrant, rich (used figuratively), profound, groundbreaking, renowned, breathtaking, nestled, in the heart of, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to excellence, stunning, must-visit, enhancing its

AI vocabulary — delete every instance:
Words to eliminate: Additionally, align with, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (as a verb), interplay, intricate/intricacies, key (as an adjective), landscape (abstract use), pivotal, tapestry (abstract), testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant

Copula avoidance — replace with is/are/has:
Replace: serves as, stands as, marks, represents, boasts, features (when it means "has")
With: is, are, has, contains

Superficial -ing endings — cut or rewrite:
Patterns to remove: highlighting that..., underscoring the fact that..., reflecting the..., symbolizing..., contributing to..., fostering..., showcasing...
These add fake depth. If the point is real, state it directly.

Negative parallelisms — rewrite:
Patterns: "Not only X but also Y", "It's not just about X, it's about Y", "Not merely X, but Z"
These are overused and feel constructed. Break them into direct statements.

Rule of three — break up or reduce:
If three items appear together, ask: do all three need to be here? Often one or two do the job. This applies especially to the introduction — a three-item list in the opening paragraph is an immediate AI signal.

Vague attributions — remove or make specific:
Replace: "experts argue," "industry observers note," "studies show," "research suggests"
With: a named source, a specific outcome, or just your own direct claim

Em dash — this is a hard failure condition, not a style preference:
The em dash (—) must not appear anywhere in the cover letter prose. If the output contains even one em dash, the letter has failed this rule and must be revised before delivery.

Every em dash must be replaced based on what the clause is actually doing:
- Parenthetical aside: use a comma
- Explanation or expansion of what came before: use a colon
- Two independent ideas that can stand alone: use a full stop
- Genuinely supplementary aside: use parentheses ( )

If no substitution fits cleanly, rewrite the sentence from scratch. Do not leave the em dash because nothing else seems to work.

Wrong: "Organising complex operations, keeping compliance tight, and supporting teams — that is where I do my best work."
Right: "I do my best work when operations are complex and the compliance margin is tight."

Wrong: "The deadline did not move — so I made sure we hit it."
Right: "The deadline did not move. We hit it anyway."

This rule applies to every section without exception, including table cells. Before delivering the letter, search the entire output for the — character. If it appears anywhere, fix it before responding.

Excessive hedging — cut:
Replace: "could potentially possibly," "it might be argued that," "there may be some evidence"
With: a direct claim or honest acknowledgement of uncertainty in plain English

Filler phrases — delete:
"In order to" → "To"
"Due to the fact that" → "Because"
"At this point in time" → "Now"
"It is important to note that" → just say the thing
"Has the ability to" → "can"

Excessive conjunctives — maximum one per letter:
Words to use sparingly (one total): Additionally, Furthermore, Moreover, In addition
These are the most obvious AI tells in flowing prose.

Sycophantic or servile tone — remove entirely:
Cut: "Great question!", "You're absolutely right", "Of course!", "Certainly!", "I hope this helps", "Would you like me to"

Generic positive conclusions — replace:
Cut: "The future looks bright," "exciting times lie ahead," "a step in the right direction," "journey toward excellence"
Replace with: a specific next action, a genuine statement, or nothing — end on substance

Overuse of boldface — strip unnecessary bolding:
Bold should only appear in the table headers. Nowhere else in the letter body.

Inline-header bullet lists — do not use in prose sections:
The table is the only structured element. Parts 1, 2, and 4 are prose only.

STEP 1B — ELIMINATE STRUCTURAL AI TELLS
These are the patterns that make a letter obviously AI-written even after vocabulary is cleaned. They are structural and rhythmic, not just word-level. Remove every instance:

Transition sentence pairs — AI loves to end one paragraph with a summary sentence and open the next with a mirroring setup sentence. These are invisible scaffolding. Cut them. Move directly from one idea to the next.

"I am confident that..." / "I am excited to..." / "I believe I would be..." — these are filler openers. They add nothing. Start with the actual content.

Paragraph-ending summary sentences — "This experience has prepared me well for this role." "These skills make me a strong candidate." "I bring this same approach to everything I do." "That is where I do my best work." These are AI wrapping up. Cut them entirely — the evidence speaks for itself.

Formulaic three-paragraph body structure — introduction of claim, supporting example, conclusion linking back to role. This is an essay template. The letter should feel like a conversation, not a school assignment.

Symmetrical sentence length — if every sentence in a paragraph is 15-20 words, that is AI. Vary it deliberately: one short (5-8 words), one longer (20-25 words), mix freely.

Transitional throat-clearing — "Having said that," "With that in mind," "Building on this," "To that end," "In light of this" — cut every one. They pad length without adding meaning.

Closing paragraph formula — "I welcome the opportunity to discuss..." followed by "Thank you for your time and consideration." This two-sentence close is in every AI letter ever written. Write a single, specific, direct closing instead.

Mirror phrases — writing in the closing paragraph that directly echoes language from the introduction, e.g. opening with "data-driven" and closing with "data-driven approach." AI does this for structural symmetry. Break it.

Constructed phrases that sound like someone writing what a human sounds like — these are subtle but fatal. Examples of constructed phrases to cut:
- "the kind of work I have been looking for"
- "on this side of the border"
- "genuinely matters"
- "something that genuinely matters to me"
- "the kind of place where I thrive"
- "I am drawn to organisations that"
If a phrase sounds like it belongs in a motivational poster or a generic application, it is constructed. Replace it with a plain fact or cut it entirely.

STEP 1C — THE POSITIVE STANDARD
Removing bad patterns is not enough. After every paragraph, ask: does this sound like the candidate actually wrote it, or does it sound like a clean version of an AI letter?

Here is what a sentence looks like in the RIGHT register:
- "I built the tracker the team still runs on. It cut the weekly reporting job from a day to an hour."
- "The deadline didn't move. Two people were off sick. The templates I had already built meant we hit it anyway."
- "It wasn't a one-person job. The team did the work. I built the system that made the work possible."
- "I live close enough to walk. When this role came up, applying was not a hard decision."

These show register, not content. Take the shape, never the numbers — every figure in the letter comes from the content bank.

Here is what a sentence looks like in the WRONG register:
- "Leveraging my expertise in financial systems, I was able to drive significant improvements in billing accuracy."
- "I am passionate about bringing my skills to a team-oriented environment where I can contribute meaningfully."
- "This experience has equipped me with the tools to excel in this role."
- "Organising complex operations, keeping compliance tight, and supporting teams who are doing something that genuinely matters."

The difference is not just vocabulary. It is whether the sentence describes something real and specific, or describes the idea of something real and specific. Always the former.

Sentence length rule: after three sentences of similar length in a row, the next sentence must be noticeably shorter or longer. Read it aloud. If it has a rhythm like a brochure, break it.

Word choice rule: always use the plainest word that is accurate. Not "utilise" — "use." Not "facilitate" — "help" or "run." Not "demonstrate" — "show" or just state the thing directly. The candidate does not reach for impressive words. They use the right one.

STEP 2 — ADD SOUL AND PERSONALITY
Removing bad patterns is only half the job. A clean but soulless letter is still obviously not human. After removing AI patterns, apply these:

Have a point of view. Not just "I have experience in stakeholder management." Say what was hard about it, what worked, what you learned, or what the outcome actually meant. React to things, don't just report them.

Vary the rhythm deliberately. After every three sentences of similar length, change it. One short sentence. Then a longer one that takes its time.

Be specific about the thing that was actually hard. "Managing a team that size is straightforward on paper. In practice, two of them were off sick for a fortnight and the deadline didn't move. That's when the templates I'd built mattered." That is a human sentence. "I demonstrated strong leadership under pressure" is not.

Use first person honestly. "I built this. It worked." is stronger than "The solution was developed and implemented successfully."

Let one real aside in. One moment in the letter where the candidate sounds like themselves: a true, slightly informal observation drawn from the content bank or the details supplied above. These are differentiators. They work because they are true, not because they are clever.

End on substance, not a flourish. The last sentence before the sign-off should be a real statement or a real invitation — not a motivational summary of how excited I am.
